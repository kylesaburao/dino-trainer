# Making rare failures rarer in ES-trained policies: risk-sensitive objectives, hard-case mining, sigma, selection and averaging

Scope: the 44-32-32-3 tanh MLP trained by OpenAI-style ES (antithetic pairs, centred ranks, Adam). Success means every one of many fresh evaluation episodes reaches the 1M cap. The shipped model reaches it on about 92% of fresh seeds. Its failures are early (464-633 points, first pterodactyls, about 4.6% of starts from 0) or late (989k-998k).

Labels. **STATED**: read in the source this session. **INFERENCE**: my reasoning from stated facts. **[D]**: computed here from a formula or simulation, with parameters given; not literature. **UNVERIFIED**: the source could not be fetched or read, or a search found nothing. This note does not repeat `es-practice.md` items 7-9 (sigma schedules, re-evaluation). It extends them.

## 1. Summary: the decision-relevant takeaways

1. **There are two different robustnesses, and sigma only buys one of them.** Sigma controls robustness to *parameter* perturbation (STATED, R7: ES optimises E over the search distribution of f, "robust to perturbation"; "if one wants ES to better mimic FD and SGD ... simply by reducing sigma"). Certification measures robustness to *seeds*. R7 explicitly leaves the link open ("remains open to further research"). HANDOFF 4.3 measured them as decoupled at sigma = 0.1: population frailty is by candidate (rho = 0.85) and not by seed (chi-square 0.93, p = 0.82); the population dies at about 70 points while the centre fails at 464-633 or at 960k+. INFERENCE: lowering sigma helps the early band most, and may do little for the late band (takeaway 2).

2. **The "100x population vs centre" gap is two different numbers late in training, which changes what sigma can fix.** HANDOFF 4.3 gives the windowed population hazard at 900k as 3.1e-5 per point (baseline theta, 20k windows, 2 seeds per level). The full-episode estimate for the baseline theta (the windowed table does not state which generation; the full-episode study used gen 970) gives 0.039 per 100k points (3.9e-7 per point) for 700k-1M. These differ by about 80x. The baseline centre's own late hazard, from the same section (3/120 crashes from 950k in 50k windows), is about 5e-7 per point [D: -ln(1-3/120)/50k]. INFERENCE (caveat: conditional-on-survival hazard is biased by frailty, and window starts may add a transient): late-band failures may be roughly *intrinsic* hazard of the policy, not sigma damage, so a sigma polish would not remove them. Reconcile the two estimates before spending runs on a late-band sigma story. This is the cheapest high-value check in the note (section 7, row 1).

3. **At small sigma the residual population failures land in the centre's early failure band; at sigma = 0.1 they do not.** HANDOFF 4.3: the curriculum theta at sigma = 0.0125 has residual crashes "at 464 points, the first pterodactyl on one seed"; at 0.1 the crashes are mostly below 100 points. Uesato et al.'s continuation method (R1) assumes "agents we train on fail in related ways to the final agent" and says otherwise it is "insufficient". INFERENCE: that assumption looks false for sigma = 0.1 perturbed copies and plausible for small-sigma copies, so any scheme that mines failures from the *population* needs small sigma, or should mine from the centre and earlier checkpoints instead. Whether the centre itself fails on that 464-point seed is not stated (UNVERIFIED).

4. **The aggregator is applied before ranking, so mean vs min vs CVaR changes only the *ordering* of per-candidate aggregates.** Centred ranks are invariant to any strictly monotone transform of the aggregate (INFERENCE from the definition of ranks; HANDOFF 4.4 states the tie behaviour). With 4 episodes per candidate, CVaR at level 25% is exactly the minimum; both rank "all four reach the cap" at the top as a big tie, which is the rank-space form of CeSoR's "tail barrier" (R5, STATED: a constant tail gives a zero gradient). It matches HANDOFF's "ffPP" median candidate. Risk aggregation is therefore not a free lunch. I found **no ES-specific CVaR/worst-case paper** (5 searches; UNVERIFIED negative). All risk-sensitive RL results found are policy-gradient (R4, R5, R6), and R5 says it is "currently not applicable to non-PG methods".

5. **Detectability is the real bottleneck, not the objective.** [D] With m episodes per candidate and per-episode failure probability q, P(see at least one failure) is 3.9% for q = 1%, m = 4, and 7.7% for m = 8; for q = 0.1% it is 0.4% and 0.8%. A candidate whose own failure rate differs by a factor of two from its neighbour is invisible to the fitness. Any remedy must raise the *density of failures per evaluated episode* (hard-case seeds, hazardous-stage windows, importance-weighted starts), not change how episodes are combined.

6. **Hard-case replay works as search, with a stated limitation, and needs a fourth seed range.** R1: replaying the initial conditions that failed in training ("prioritized replay" adversary) was often much cheaper than random testing, but "an agent may learn to handle the particular problem instances on which it was trained, without eliminating all possible failures" (this happened once on Humanoid). PLR (R2) mixes score-based and staleness-based sampling to avoid exactly that drift. Mine only from a dedicated range; never recycle validation or certification seeds (HANDOFF 9.11, section 3.2 below).

7. **Importance sampling gives unbiased failure-rate estimates, not zero-failure certificates.** R1 Proposition 3.2: the variance-minimising proposal has density proportional to the square root of the failure probability times the base density. Useful for *finding* failures and ranking checkpoints that all show zero failures under plain Monte Carlo (R1: 36 of 50 Humanoid checkpoints "never failed" under plain Monte Carlo, so could not be ranked). INFERENCE: do not use it for the final certificate; the rule-of-three semantics are lost (a bound after zero observed failures needs a bound on importance weights, which I did not find in a source).

8. **Selection under noise is a winner's-curse problem; budget it, then certify on fresh seeds.** [D, parameters in section 5] Picking the best of K = 20 checkpoints by 36 validation seeds, with true pass rates drawn from Beta(mean 0.90, sd 0.03), shows an average observed 0.987 against a true 0.922, and a 56% chance the winner scores 36/36 by luck (analytic: 1-(1-0.92^36)^20 = 0.64 if every checkpoint were exactly 0.92). Multiplicity costs: 0 failures in N fresh games certifies <= 1% at 95% for N = 299 with one finalist and N = 408 with three (Bonferroni, [D]). Hoeffding races (R10) are too loose for rare binary failures (half-width 0.226 at n = 36); use Clopper-Pearson (already in `rules.js`).

9. **Weight averaging is the one no-retrain option that ships unchanged, but evidence for it in ES is absent.** SWA in A2C/DDPG (R16) raised mean final reward on 4 of 6 Atari games (Seaquest within noise, CrazyClimber lower) and on all 4 MuJoCo tasks (3 repeats, mostly within one standard deviation), over a short continued run after pre-training (STATED). Soups (R17) average models from a shared initialisation in one basin; greedy soup is by construction no worse than the best single model on the selection set. I found no paper on weight averaging or Polyak averaging of ES iterates (UNVERIFIED negative). INFERENCE: averaging the last k checkpoints of one run keeps the 44-32-32-3 format, so `export` and `export-css` need no change; action-vote ensembles would not.

10. **Recommended order of cheap tests.** (a) Reconcile the two population hazard estimates (takeaway 2). (b) Resume near the end with small sigma and a tie-controlled sigma, certify on fresh seeds. (c) Average the last k checkpoints with a lambda sweep, certify. (d) Only then hard-case mining and risk-aggregated fitness, which need code. Every arm needs at least 3 runs to separate effects below about 0.3 (HANDOFF 8); one run per arm detects only effects of at least 0.4 in pass rate.

## 2. Q1: risk-sensitive and worst-case objectives with ES / black-box RL

### 2.1 What the literature offers (all policy-gradient unless marked)
- **CVaR and coherent risk.**
  - STATED (R6, Tamar et al. 2015): the risk-sensitive policy gradient extends to the whole class of coherent risk measures; CVaR is "the mean of the alpha-tail distribution of Z" for continuous Z; each coherent measure has a "risk envelope" (a set of reweightings of the distribution), which is the distributionally robust reading of CVaR. Their CVaR gradient formula uses only trajectories beyond the quantile.
  - INFERENCE: the envelope view means CVaR *is* a distributionally robust objective over reweightings of the seed distribution with density ratio at most 1/alpha. A "DRO over seeds" is therefore the same family as CVaR over seeds here. I did not find a DRO-for-ES paper (UNVERIFIED negative).
- **EPOpt (R4, Rajeswaran et al.).** STATED: optimise CVaR over an ensemble of simulator models; in each iteration sample N trajectories, compute the epsilon-percentile of returns, update the policy only on trajectories at or below it (Algorithm 1, lines 7-9); a baseline fitted on the subset is "crucial for the CVaR case" (the unbaselined subset gradient is biased; Appendix); with small epsilon from the start "learning was unstable", so the authors run epsilon = 1 first and then switch (their appendix experiment: 100 iterations at epsilon = 1, then epsilon = 0.1). Evaluation used the 10th percentile of returns as the worst-case proxy.
- **CeSoR (R5, Greenberg et al., NeurIPS 2022).** STATED: standard CVaR policy gradient ignores successful trajectories because they never fall in the worst alpha-fraction; they define a *tail barrier* (a constant-valued tail) and prove that if the tail barrier is wider than alpha the gradient is zero with high probability (Theorem 1, Hoeffding-based). Remedies: (i) *soft risk*: start with alpha' near 1 and decrease it to alpha over the first rho*M steps; (ii) a *cross-entropy sampler* that oversamples training contexts that lead to low returns, with importance weights. Limitations stated: needs partial control over the training conditions via a parametric family, and "currently not applicable to non-PG methods".
- **ES-specific uses.** I found none for CVaR, lower quantiles or min-aggregation in ES/neuroevolution, nor an Uber "worst-case evaluation" paper (queries tried: ES + CVaR, risk-averse CMA-ES, worst-case CMA-ES, ES + risk-sensitive RL, Uber worst-case; UNVERIFIED negative; only noise-handling CMA-ES results appeared). The nearest ES-side practice already in `es-practice.md`: Such et al. re-evaluate the top 10 on 30 extra episodes, and Hardmaru averages 16 rollouts per candidate and reports a more robust final policy at "16x worse" data efficiency (R19, summary only).

### 2.2 How these interact with rank-based fitness shaping (INFERENCE unless noted)
1. Order of operations in the trainer: per-candidate scalar `F_i = agg(episode scores)`, then `centredRanks`, then `esGradient`. Ranks discard scale. So "CVaR instead of mean" matters only when it reorders candidates.
2. Which reorderings can matter with m = 4 (or 8) episodes: the minimum, or any CVaR at alpha <= 1/m, reduces to the minimum; the lexicographic order (min, then mean) or a mean of a concave transform u(score) are the only variants that break ties within the all-pass group by something other than noise. Mean of ln(1+score) is already the `--select count` tiebreak in `rules.js` (HANDOFF 5.2); it is a validation rule, not a training fitness.
3. Magnitude is irrelevant to ranks, so the "late failure at 990k costs only 1% of an episode under a linear score" worry does not bite: a candidate with episode scores (1,1,1,0.99) still ranks below (1,1,1,1) if they are not tied. The loss is in the *ties*: with a 34% opening atom (HANDOFF 4.3) and all-pass candidates tied at the top, ranks carry little information, whatever the aggregator.
4. Tail barrier in rank form: min-over-4 sorts most candidates into "one episode died at about 70 points", a big tie at the bottom of the order, the mirror of the top tie. With `--rank-ties index` that tie becomes a random walk (HANDOFF 4.2); with `mid` it carries zero weight. A CeSoR-style soft schedule (the EPOpt warm-up too) maps to: start with the mean (alpha' = 1) while most candidates fail the opening, and move to a lower-tail aggregate only after the opening saturates. This matches HANDOFF 9.4's gated/lexicographic fitness, which is a risk-aware rule: sort by (opening result, then late result).
5. Pair-difference form (`pairDiffWeights`, ARS style, HANDOFF 4.4): aggregating *within* a pair, e.g. weight on min(F+) - min(F-), is the only place where a lower-tail statistic keeps a signal when most candidates tie globally, because the weight depends on discordant pairs only.

### 2.3 Verdict for this project
Changing the aggregator is unlikely to be the lever (takeaways 4-5). The useful pieces are (a) a staged or soft schedule (EPOpt/CeSoR), already close to HANDOFF 9.4, and (b) CeSoR's *sampler*, i.e. oversampling hard contexts (section 3).

## 3. Q2: hard-case mining, prioritised replay, importance sampling of rare events

### 3.1 Sources
- **Prioritized Level Replay (R2, Jiang et al. 2021).** STATED: each level has a score (best-performing: L1 value loss) and a last-sampled timestamp. The replay distribution is `P_replay = (1 - rho) * P_S + rho * P_C`; `P_S` uses rank prioritisation `h(S_i) = 1/rank(S_i)` with temperature beta; `P_C` is proportional to staleness `c - C_i`. A Bernoulli decision picks between replaying a seen level and a new unseen one. The headline Procgen result uses rank prioritisation with beta = 0.1 and rho = 0.1; on MiniGrid the paper reports a staleness coefficient of rho = 0.3 as best for test performance. The score needs value estimates; in ES, a failure indicator or fraction-of-population-failing replaces it (INFERENCE).
- **Robust PLR (R3, Jiang et al. 2021b).** STATED (abstract): stopping policy updates on uncurated (random) levels gives a robustness guarantee at Nash equilibria and better out-of-distribution results. INFERENCE: for ES the analogue is that candidates are scored on the curated hard pool only, which is exactly the "Run B" hazard (HANDOFF 6.3: a mean over windows can hide a failed opening). Keep a stratum of unmined episodes.
- **Adversarial/failure-directed evaluation (R1, Uesato et al. 2018).** STATED: initial condition x with outcome C = c(x, Z); the failure-probability predictor `f*(x) = P(C = 1 | x)`; the adversary samples n conditions from the base distribution and runs the one with highest predicted f; the risk estimator is importance sampling with proposal density `q_f` proportional to `f^(1/2) p_X` (Proposition 3.2; a hyperparameter alpha replaces 1/2 for robustness to errors in f), unbiased for any f bounded away from zero. The predictor is trained on data from *earlier training iterations*, which fail more often "in related ways". Reported: failures found with 198x (driving) and 3100x (Humanoid) fewer episodes in the median case; on Humanoid the random adversary needs over 300,000 episodes for a 95% chance of one failure, more than the training budget.
- **Rare-event simulation and the cross-entropy method (R9, de Boer et al. 2005; Rubinstein 1997, R22, cited from it, UNVERIFIED as a primary source).** STATED (R9): crude Monte Carlo is unworkable when the probability is below about 1e-5; importance sampling reweights by a likelihood ratio; the CE algorithm builds a *sequence of levels* gamma_t from the (1-rho)-quantile of the performance S under the current sampling density and refits the density to the elite samples, until the real threshold is reached; the final estimate uses the likelihood ratio. Greenberg et al. (R5) use this to sample "the worst contexts" in RL.

### 3.2 Applying it here (INFERENCE)
- **What is a "level"?** A seed is a level only if its hazard is a property of the seed. HANDOFF 4.3 finds no seed frailty for the population (chi-square 0.93, p = 0.82), but that is for sigma-perturbed copies and for failures dominated by the opening atom. For the *centre*, the early failures cluster at the first pterodactyls at speed about 8.5 (HANDOFF 4.3, 4.2), so the hazard depends on game state at 464-633 points, which depends on the seed through the obstacle stream. Two natural levels: (i) a seed, replayed from 0; (ii) a (seed, startScore) pair replayed from a window (HANDOFF 2.3: `startScore` is faithful from about 2k points; below 2k the speed is wrong, so the 464-633 band must be replayed from 0, with episodes capped at about 700 points via `--start-zero-cap`).
- **Cheap mining.** Run the *centre* (and a few late checkpoints) from 0 with a 700-point cap on many *training-range* seeds; at about 4.6% failure that yields roughly 1 failing seed per 22 episodes, so 2,200 episodes give about 100 failing seeds for very little compute (a 700-point episode is short). For the late band, windows from 950k to 1M on training-range seeds (HANDOFF 4.3 measured 3/120 from 950k). Keep these in a pool.
- **Sampling from the pool.** PLR form: with probability p_replay draw from the pool (rank-prioritised by failure count of recent centres and checkpoints, with a staleness term so old failures are re-checked as the policy changes), else a fresh seed. Importance weights are unnecessary for *training* (the gradient is a heuristic ascent on a shifted objective, as for any curriculum); they are necessary only if the replay stratum is used to *estimate* a failure rate.
- **Do not contaminate (the key constraint).** Four disjoint ranges: (1) ordinary training seeds, (2) the mining pool drawn from range (1)'s own seed space (or a fifth range, but never from 3 or 4), (3) validation seeds (checkpoint selection), (4) held-out/certification seeds (`EVAL_SEEDS`). Rules: failures found on validation or held-out seeds are *never* added to the pool (HANDOFF 9.11: the statistics agent's four hard seeds came from a fresh 50-seed test set, which would stop being fresh); if some are used, retire that set as a test set. After mining and training on a pool, certify at an offset never used for anything (`eval --seed-offset`). Selection by "best of K tested on the same 25 seeds" already inflates alpha to K(1 - eps)^25 (HANDOFF 4.4).
- **Anti-cheat distinction.** The AGENTS.md rules (observations use only obstacles with x < 600; never observe `o.gap`, `requirement()`/`placeFairly()`, RNG state, spawn history, difficulty internals) bind what the *policy observes*. A training-side scheduler that inspects the generated obstacle stream, or just records which seeds the centre failed on, does not change the policy's observation. INFERENCE; flag it to the project owner, as the line between "scheduler reads the future" and "policy reads the future" is a project decision.
- **Known failure mode.** Replayed instances can be memorised or merely patched (R1, STATED); keep the staleness mixing, keep a majority of fresh seeds, and judge on fresh seeds only.
- **Importance-sampled starts.** HANDOFF 4.4 already derives start densities (uniform in points is within 5-10% of optimal for the population hazard). R1's `f^(1/2)` has the same square-root shape; no new start distribution is needed unless a predictor of failure by (seed, start) exists.

## 4. Q3: smaller sigma late in training; J_sigma versus F

### 4.1 Theory (STATED, then INFERENCE)
- STATED (R8, Nesterov and Spokoiny, Gaussian smoothing `f_mu(x) = E f(x + mu u)`): if f is Lipschitz with constant L0, then `|f_mu(x) - f(x)| <= mu L0 sqrt(n)` (Theorem 1, eq. 18); if the gradient is Lipschitz with L1, then `|f_mu - f| <= (mu^2/2) L1 n` (eq. 19); for twice-smooth f, `f_mu - f` equals `(mu^2/2)` times the trace of the Hessian up to an `O(mu^3)` remainder (eq. 20); gradient error `<= (mu/2) L1 (n+3)^(3/2)` (Lemma 3, eq. 27) and `<= (mu^2/6) L2 (n+4)^2` (eq. 28). Here n is the dimension.
- STATED (R7, Lehman et al. 2018): ES's objective is `J(theta) = E_{z ~ pi(.|theta)} f(z)`; in Salimans-style ES "the evolved distributional parameters control only the mean ... and not its variance ... ES will be driven towards robust areas of the search space"; in toy landscapes sigma = 0.18 crosses a zero-fitness gap that smaller sigma cannot ("Gradient Gap"), sigma = 0.16 ignores local optima in "Fleeting Peaks" while sigma = 0.002 "latches onto the first"; in Humanoid, ES is more robust than a GA or TRPO by the measure (median perturbed performance / unperturbed performance, 1,000 perturbations each averaged over 100 trials; ES sigma = 0.02). They also state a counter-case, "Narrowing Path", where high-sigma ES is less attractive "if the ultimate goal is a single solution (and there is no concern about its robustness)".
- INFERENCE: the Nesterov-Spokoiny bounds do not apply as written. Here F(theta) = P(reach cap) is a piecewise-constant function of the weights for a fixed seed (a crash is a threshold event), averaged over seeds it is only as smooth as the seed mixture makes it. The `mu^2` law is the right *shape* where a Hessian exists: `J_sigma - F` shrinks like sigma^2 for a smooth mean F, and with `L0`/`L1` effectively unbounded near a crash boundary the bound is vacuous.
- HANDOFF 4.3's sigma sweep is the real evidence. Failure fraction before 2k points (1 minus the surviving share): curriculum theta 61%, 14%, 4%, 2% at sigma = 0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125; baseline theta 15%, 0.3%, 0% at 0.1, 0.05, 0.025. [D] Ratios per halving: 4.4x, 3.5x, 2x for the curriculum model (sigma^2 would be 4x, then flattening at a floor), and 50x for the baseline between 0.1 and 0.05. So halving sigma buys at least a quadratic reduction in perturbed-copy opening failures until a floor set by failures the centre itself has.

### 4.2 When does smaller sigma help the *mean* policy? (INFERENCE)
- It closes the gap between what ES scores (perturbed copies) and what is deployed (the centre), because `J_sigma -> F` as sigma -> 0 (R8, eq. 18-20 shape). This only matters if ES's choices among candidates are being distorted by sigma damage, i.e. in the early band (takeaway 3).
- Costs: (i) more ties, since fewer perturbed copies change the outcome (HANDOFF 9.8: "smaller sigma raises plateau ties", and `--sigma-anneal` "fights a tie controller"); (ii) loss of the robustness-seeking and gap-crossing properties (R7); (iii) for antithetic estimates, the signal comes from *discordant* pairs on shared seeds; at tiny sigma most pairs are concordant, so the gradient is dominated by the few seeds near a crash boundary. That is also where the information on rare failures lives, which is a reason to prefer a *controlled* sigma: HANDOFF 9.1's tie-rate controller `ln sigma_{t+1} = ln sigma_t + kappa (q_t - q*)` with q* = 0.2 targets the sigma at which a fixed share of pairs is discordant, rather than a fixed schedule.
- The late band (989k-998k) is unlikely to respond (takeaway 2). A smaller sigma cannot remove failures that the centre has at a hazard comparable to the survivors' hazard.
- Related prior art not repeated: fixed vs annealed sigma, multiplicative decay to a floor, Such et al.'s anneal to 0.001 with "a small performance boost at the end" (`es-practice.md` item 7).

## 5. Q4: selection under noise for rare failures

### 5.1 Racing
- STATED (R10, Maron and Moore 1994): a race keeps a Hoeffding interval `eps = sqrt(B^2 log(2/delta) / (2n))` for each model's mean error, eliminates models whose best possible error is worse than the best model's worst possible error, and gets overall confidence by a union bound over `n` iterations and `m` models, `delta = Delta/(n m)`. "Hoeffding Races never does worse than brute force" and are least effective when all models are close.
- Heidrich-Meisner and Igel (R11, ICML 2009): Hoeffding and empirical-Bernstein races inside CMA-ES to set the number of episodes per candidate so the estimate is "just accurate enough to rank". UNVERIFIED beyond the abstract; six URLs tried (ICML, ACM, author sites, mlanthology) returned HTML, not the paper. No claim about their episode limits or confidence levels is made here.
- Empirical Bernstein stopping (R20, Mnih et al. 2008): only a search snippet was read (S); it says Bernstein bounds need fewer samples than Hoeffding because the Hoeffding width does not depend on variance. For binary pass/fail with rare failures the variance is tiny (p(1-p) near 0.01), so an exact binomial interval is the natural fit.
- [D] Hoeffding half-width at delta = 0.05 for a [0,1] variable: 0.392 at n = 12, 0.226 at n = 36, 0.136 at n = 100, 0.043 at n = 1000. Useless for separating 92% from 99%. Clopper-Pearson one-sided 95% upper bound on the failure rate after 0 failures: 0.221 (n = 12), 0.080 (36), 0.050 (59), 0.030 (100), 0.0198 (150), 0.010 (299); the rule of three (3/n) is within 5% of it from n = 59. `clopperPearson` and `sequentialAllPass` already exist in `trainer/rules.js`.
- Practical race here: a candidate checkpoint is dropped as soon as its first failure appears (all-pass is the target), which is what `sequentialAllPass` does. For a two-checkpoint comparison on the same fresh seeds, use McNemar on the discordant seeds (HANDOFF 8).

### 5.2 Sequential probability ratio test
- STATED (Oxford lecture notes, R12, via search snippet only): approximate SPRT boundaries `A = log(beta/(1-alpha))` and `B = log((1-beta)/alpha)`; decide H1 when the cumulative log-likelihood ratio reaches B and H0 when it falls to A. The notes derive the Bernoulli case. The sign convention differs between sources, so check before implementing.
- [D] With bad failure rate p1 and good rate p0, alpha = beta = 0.05, each pass adds `ln((1-p1)/(1-p0))` (negative, toward "good") and each failure adds `ln(p1/p0)`; boundaries are +/-2.944. Passes needed to accept "good" with no failure: p0 = 1%, p1 = 5% gives 72 (matches HANDOFF 4.4); p0 = 0.5%, p1 = 5% gives 64; p0 = 0.5%, p1 = 3% gives 116; p0 = 0.2%, p1 = 2% gives 162. One failure adds +1.609 (1% vs 5%) or +2.303 (0.5% vs 5%), so it costs about 39-50 passes to recover from, not an automatic rejection.
- Value here: as HANDOFF says, it saves nothing for a good model (still about 64-72 straight passes) and early rejection is already in `sequentialAllPass`. Its use is the *middle* case: a checkpoint with one early failure in 20 games need not be discarded if later games pass. The cost is that it fixes p0 and p1, which should be set by the certification target (for example, "good" = 0.5%, "bad" = 5%).

### 5.3 Winner's curse and multiple comparisons
- STATED (R13, Smith and Winkler 2006, abstract via search): picking the top-ranked option from unbiased noisy estimates makes the realised value fall short of the estimate ("optimizer's curse"); Bayesian adjustment is the proposed remedy. A 2007 correction notice states that the "expected disappointment" figures in their Figure 3 were wrong (a simulation error) and are "substantially larger"; qualitative conclusions stand. UNVERIFIED beyond the abstract and correction summary.
- STATED (R14, Colas et al.): running many experiments inflates the false-positive rate "linearly with the number of experiments"; use a correction for multiple comparisons such as Bonferroni, `alpha_Bon = alpha/N_E`.
- [D, computed here with `stats.py`] Simulation of "best of K checkpoints by n = 36 validation seeds", true pass rates `p_i ~ Beta` with mean 0.90 and sd 0.03, 1,500 trials, ties broken at random:

  | K | Mean observed pass rate of winner | Mean true pass rate of winner | P(winner scores 36/36) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | 0.899 | 0.901 | 0.031 |
  | 5 | 0.960 | 0.916 | 0.187 |
  | 20 | 0.987 | 0.922 | 0.565 |
  | 100 | 1.000 | 0.928 | 0.986 |

  Chance of all-pass for a checkpoint with exactly p = 0.92: 12 seeds 0.368, 36 seeds 0.050, 59 seeds 0.0073. So "a checkpoint passed all 36 validation seeds" is weak evidence when many were tried. The sim assumes independent Beta true rates; real checkpoints along one run are correlated, so the winner's advantage is a bound for a spread-out sequence, not a point prediction.
- [D] Seeds needed for 0 failures to certify failure rate <= eps at 95% when a Bonferroni correction covers K finalists (`N = ceil(ln(0.05/K)/ln(1-eps))`):

  | eps | K = 1 | K = 3 | K = 10 | K = 20 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 5% | 59 | 80 | 104 | 117 |
  | 2% | 149 | 203 | 263 | 297 |
  | 1% | 299 | 408 | 528 | 597 |
  | 0.5% | 598 | 817 | 1,058 | 1,196 |

  Matches HANDOFF 4.4 for K = 1 (59, 149, 299).
- R1 (STATED): its Humanoid model-selection experiment shows the same tie problem: under plain Monte Carlo "36 out of 50 policies have never failed", so "the experimenter would be forced to choose one of these with no further information"; the adversarial estimator ranked them and picked a policy over 3 times more robust (conservative estimate).

### 5.4 Practical recipe (INFERENCE built on the above)
1. **Three stages, three seed sets.** Stage A: cheap screen with `--fold-validation` and 12 seeds every generation (HANDOFF 9.6). Stage B: a finalist set of at most K = 3 checkpoints (distinct generations, not neighbours) on a second set of at least 59 seeds, with early abort at the first failure (`--stop-rule allpass:59`). Stage C: the single survivor on fresh certification seeds at a never-used offset, N chosen from the table above for the target eps and the number of finalists.
2. **Never certify on seeds that influenced selection.** Under `allpass:59`, held-out indices 0..58 become stop seeds (HANDOFF 9.6); certify from offset 100 or above, as HANDOFF notes.
3. **Report the count of looks.** Every extra look at the same seeds multiplies alpha (HANDOFF 4.4: `K (1 - eps)^25`).
4. **Prefer ranking checkpoints by a continuous but unbiased signal when all pass.** For example the Clopper-Pearson upper bound on window-level hazard from additional capped windows, or an importance-weighted failure estimate (R1), because at 36 or 59 all-pass seeds the pass counts cannot rank.
5. **Use paired comparisons between two finalists** (same seeds, McNemar on discordant seeds); 125 seeds give +/-0.09 on a pass-rate difference (HANDOFF 8).

## 6. Q5: ensembling and checkpoint averaging without retraining

### 6.1 Evidence
- **SWA (R15, Izmailov et al. 2018).** STATED: averaging SGD iterates "with a cyclical or high constant learning rate" finds "much flatter solutions" and generalises better; the averaged model is a single network. (Their setting is supervised learning; also see `es-practice.md`'s warning that ES iterates behave differently.)
- **SWA in RL (R16, Nikishin et al., a 2018 workshop paper).** STATED: SWA applied to A2C and DDPG stabilises solutions, "alleviates the problem of forgetting the highly rewarded policy", and improves average reward on several Atari and MuJoCo environments. Their method keeps a separate averaged copy of the weights, "which does not influence the optimization process"; the average starts from a model pre-trained for a fixed number of steps; update frequency c = 100 updates (A2C Atari) or 2,000 timesteps (DDPG). Table 1 (3 repeats): Breakout 522 to 703, Qbert 18,777 to 21,272, SpaceInvaders 7,727 to 21,676 (sd 8,897), BeamRider 9,999 to 11,321, Seaquest 1,779 to 1,795 (within noise), **CrazyClimber 147,030 to 139,752 (lower)**. So the effect is not uniform. They cite Polyak and Juditsky (1992, R21) for the convex-optimisation rate (UNVERIFIED as a primary source) and call a theory for RL "future work".
- **Model soups (R17, Wortsman et al. 2022).** STATED: models "optimized independently from the same pre-trained initialization" lie in the same low-error basin; the uniform soup can lose to the best single model; the greedy soup adds models one at a time only if held-out accuracy does not drop, so "the greedy soup can be no worse than the best single model" on the held-out set used to build it. Ensembles often do slightly better than soups on ImageNet in their pair-wise analysis but cost k forward passes.
- **Rewarded soups (R18, Rame et al.).** STATED (abstract): weights fine-tuned on different rewards from a shared pre-trained initialization "remain linearly connected" and can be interpolated; they report control (locomotion) tasks among the domains. Preference interpolation rather than variance reduction, so weak evidence here.
- **ES specifically.** No paper found on weight averaging or Polyak averaging of ES iterates, or on SWA for neuroevolution (UNVERIFIED negative; two searches). The ES update itself is already an average over a perturbation population (STATED, Salimans-style ES, R7), and Adam adds momentum, so the iterate path is a smoothed trajectory; whether a further average of it helps is open.

### 6.2 What it would do here (INFERENCE)
- If late checkpoints sit in the same basin (successive checkpoints of one run share an initialisation, the soup condition), their mean may lie nearer the middle of the flat region. For a tanh MLP there is no batch norm to recompute, which removes SWA's main extra step.
- Mechanism for fewer rare failures: a rare crash is often one marginal decision (jump release time, duck timing) that checkpoints disagree on; an average trades their idiosyncratic margins for a smoother decision surface. This is a hypothesis; the Nikishin CrazyClimber result shows it can also lose.
- Format: averaging keeps 44-32-32-3, so `export`, `export-css`, `test/net.test.js` and the CSS page need no change. An action-vote ensemble of k nets needs k forward passes per frame in JS and cannot be expressed in the CSS network page without redesign; logit averaging across k nets is also not the shipped format. Prefer weight averaging.
- Safety: never overwrite the shipped `model.js` or `model.css` while testing; write the averaged theta into a new run directory (AGENTS.md: `--auto-export` overwrites the shipped model; `net.test.js` checks bit-identical decisions for the shipped model).

## 8. Open questions

1. Are the late failures (989k-998k) σ-damage-free? The two population hazard estimates in HANDOFF 4.3 differ by about 80x late; resolving this decides whether any sigma or objective change can touch the late band (takeaway 2, row 1).
2. Is the hazard at the first pterodactyls a property of the seed (obstacle stream) or only of the state at that moment? If only of the state, windows from 0 capped near 700 points are the right "level"; if seed-specific, replay by seed.
3. With 4 episodes per candidate, can any aggregator or sampler raise discordant-pair density enough to move rare failures? [D] arithmetic says detection is 4% at q = 1%; a pool in which candidates fail at 20% per episode would make it 1-(0.8)^4 = 59% [D]. Whether a pool of centre-failing seeds yields a 20% failure rate for sigma-perturbed candidates is untested.
4. Does a concave utility on the capped score (so that a crash at 500 points costs more than at 990k) change the rank order often enough to matter? Not measured.
5. Does ES-iterate averaging beat the last checkpoint at all? No ES-specific evidence found. With Adam, the final iterate may sit off-centre of a flat region by a noise-sized step.
6. Do mined failures transfer across checkpoints (Uesato's assumption) at the small sigma values used late? Needs a fail-rate check of the pool on later checkpoints.
7. Whether the scheduler inspecting the obstacle stream is acceptable under the project's anti-cheat spirit (section 3.2): a project-owner decision.
8. The importance-sampling certificate: what bound is valid after 0 failures with weights? Not found in sources read.
9. Heidrich-Meisner and Igel's race parameters (UNVERIFIED), whether their uncertainty handling in CMA-ES transfers to rank-based ES with antithetic pairs.
